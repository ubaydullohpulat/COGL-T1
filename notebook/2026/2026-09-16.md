### Summary
Built SwarmLab v0, a Monte Carlo emulator of Ling-3.0-flash split across 2–8 home peers, and swept 3 topologies × 6 link profiles.
Over home internet, network time is ~95% of a token, so throughput is set by how many sequential network crossings a token needs.
A pipeline across a few peers gives ~2–4 tok/s at 137 ms. Naively sharding experts across peers (a round trip in every MoE layer)
gives ~0.12 tok/s, 20–30× worse, even though its per-peer memory is lowest. That makes the project's core bet conditional:
expert-level sharding works only if routing locality lets most layers avoid a remote round trip. Real routing traces must now answer that.
Prefill is bandwidth-bound: ~9 s for a 1k-token prompt over a 4-peer chain at bf16, ~5 s at int8.

## Session 2

**Phase / test:** S02 — real routing + measured compute on Ling-3.0-tiny; T1-lite gate; SwarmLab v1 replay
**Session goal (agreed):** Researcher approved P-10 ("yes go ahead"): set up MLX, get Ling-3.0-tiny, measure compute, log real routing, replay in SwarmLab with a hybrid topology.

### Summary
The environment is up and Ling-3.0-tiny runs on the M4 Max after patching the community weights. On 6,136 real decode tokens,
routing is clearly structured: top-25% experts carry 57% of traffic, adjacent tokens share 44% of experts (random: 6%),
and same-domain prompts use similar experts. **T1-lite gate: PASS.** But replaying that routing with measured compute over
home links shows static hot-expert caching is too leaky. A stage keeping 90% of its experts still drops from 2.48 to
0.91 tok/s at 137 ms, because any layer missing any of its 8 experts costs a round trip. Expert sharding over home internet
needs per-layer miss rates of ~1–3.5%, and static caches give ~18–66%. Sequence-specific caches are far better (oracle 0.81 all-local at 50%),
which points to session-adaptive placement or expert paging as the next thing to test.
